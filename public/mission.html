Gale Crater: where to land, and why

Three candidate landing sites, five boxwork clusters, ten traverses. This page explains why site A is our recommendation, why site C is the high-reward alternative, and where every number comes from.

What we are looking for

The mission targets boxwork formations in Gale Crater — networks of thin ridges that record fluid flow through fractures in the bedrock. We mapped five clusters, lettered A to E, with twenty sub-regions inside them, 16.3 km² in total. The largest is cluster D at 7.5 km²; the smallest are A and E, each under half a square kilometre.

All five sit between about −3.3 km and −3.8 km of elevation, on the floor of the crater and the lower slopes of the mound. That matters for the landing choice, because a site that is already high on the mound starts the traverse closer to the clusters.

Landing site A — our recommendation

Site A is the most promising of the three. It lies close to the ground Curiosity has already driven, which makes its terrain the best studied of any candidate: we are not guessing from orbit alone. From A a rover can also reach the boxwork formation that Curiosity itself found, which gives the mission a known target rather than an inferred one.

The site is not steep. It is essentially a plain with a slope of about 3°, gentle enough that a rover can travel out of it in any direction without a difficult descent or climb.

One caveat: the surface at A is not fully characterised at rover scale. The regional slope is friendly, but individual rocks, sand pockets and scarps still have to be checked by the descent camera or a precursor orbiter pass before touchdown.

Traverses from A are short and moderate: 3.1 km to cluster D, 3.5 km to E, and 7.6 km to C, each with a net climb of roughly 375–470 m. That is a few sols of driving for a Curiosity-class rover, and it can be done one cluster at a time with a return to the landing site between them.

Landing site C — most science, most risk

Site C is the worst of the three in terms of safety, and the best in terms of science per kilometre driven. It provides the shortest routes to the boxwork formations: 1.4 km to clusters B and C, 2.7 km to A and 4.1 km to D. All four traverses run downhill, losing between 110 and 340 m of elevation, so the rover arrives at the clusters with its energy budget still intact.

The price is terrain. C sits about a kilometre higher than A, on the flank of the mound, in an area 19.6 km² in size. It contains hills with slopes up to 17°, though most of its surface is 3–4°, the same as at A. Those hills are the risk: a landing ellipse that touches one is a landing ellipse that can fail, and the rougher ground also slows the first drive out.

If the descent and the first traverse are executed correctly, C returns more science than A — four clusters inside 4.1 km instead of three inside 7.6 km, all of them downhill.

Landing site B — the reserve

B is the lowest of the three at −4.47 km mean elevation and covers 7.5 km². Its mapped routes climb 595–655 m to reach clusters A and B, the longest net climbs of any site, and it gives access to two clusters rather than three or four. We keep it as a fallback: it is a valid site, but it asks the rover to do more work for less coverage.

Recommendation

  • Primary: site A. Best-studied terrain, a flat plain, access to the boxwork formation Curiosity found, and three clusters within 8 km.
  • Ambitious alternative: site C. Shortest traverses and the highest science return, if the hills at the edge of the ellipse and the rougher surface are accepted.
  • Fallback: site B. Valid but the weakest of the three: longest climbs, fewest clusters.

The choice comes down to one trade: certainty of landing versus distance to the science. A buys certainty, C buys kilometres.
